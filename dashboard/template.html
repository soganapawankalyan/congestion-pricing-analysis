Did congestion pricing really cut traffic?

New York began charging most vehicles to enter Manhattan below 60th Street on January 5, 2025. Using only public bridge-and-tunnel counts and the zone's own entry data, I asked three questions: how much traffic left, did drivers change when they travel, and did traffic move to other roads.

Independent portfolio project. Data are public (MTA open data on data.ny.gov). The MTA is mentioned for context only and is not affiliated with this work.

1. How much traffic left?

Each week at the two tunnels that feed the zone is compared with the same week of the year in 2023–24, relative to far-away crossings that were never tolled. Those crossings show how much a week can drift by chance.

What this analysis cannot say

  • Only two crossings are treated. They are the only bridge-and-tunnel counts that feed the zone, so results are noisy and cannot be read as a zone-wide effect.
  • The toll is partly dampened at the tunnels. Some drivers get crossing credits there, so effects at these crossings are likely smaller than elsewhere.
  • No pre-period for the zone file. It starts with the toll, so zone-wide totals can be compared only with outside baselines, not with a "before".
  • Donor crossings may have been affected too. If never-tolled crossings absorbed some diverted traffic, the tunnel effect is understated.
  • The 05:00 price rise cannot be isolated. Early-morning commuting and deliveries ramp up around then anyway.
  • Diversion checks are descriptive. A small rise at a nearby bridge fits diversion but does not prove it.

Method: gap estimator against a donor index with placebo tests in space and time; price-boundary jump estimate on a log scale with a fake-boundary benchmark. Code, tests and every number are in the repository.

Data: MTA Bridges and Tunnels hourly crossings and MTA Congestion Relief Zone vehicle entries, both from New York State open data. Independent work; not affiliated with the MTA or any agency.
